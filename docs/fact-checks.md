# Fact-checks for Second Rush

Research pass for issue #3. Each item lists the claim as the game uses it (in `design.md` or `text-game/src/scenes/*.ts`), a verdict, the evidence, and a recommended change. Nothing here changes the plot; these are recommendations only.

Verdicts: **confirmed** (the claim holds as written), **partly** (the core is right but a detail is off or overstated), **wrong** (the claim as written is inaccurate), **unknown** (could not verify; says what would settle it).

A note on method: research was done by web search. Direct page fetches were blocked in this session's network, so most evidence comes from search-result excerpts of the cited pages, not full reads. Court opinions, USGS/BLM/NPS pages and the Tanana Chiefs Conference site were preferred. Before any of this goes into marketing copy or an in-game "historical note", spot-check the cited URLs.

## High priority

### 1. Native land freeze dates

**Claim.** Act 2's Tanana Chiefs meeting (October 1967) is "about the land freeze, the claims". `design.md` describes a "federal freeze on land transfers pending Native claims (mid/late 1960s)".

**Verdict.** Confirmed, with nuance.

**Evidence.**
- Interior Secretary Stewart Udall halted transfers of land under Native protest by administrative action, usually dated December 1, 1966 (NPS, *Alaska Subsistence*, ch. 4: https://www.nps.gov/parkhistory/online_books/norris1/chap4.htm). Some popular sources say 1965; 1966 is the standard date.
- The informal freeze was formalized as Public Land Order 4582, 34 Fed. Reg. 1025, in January 1969. Sources differ on the exact day (ISER's 1971 paper says January 20, 1969: https://iseralaska.org/static/legacy_publication_links/1971-LandFreeze.pdf; the Federal Register page is 34 FR 1025, published January 23, 1969).
- ANCSA revoked PLO 4582 in December 1971 (43 U.S.C. 1616(d): https://law.justia.com/codes/us/2001/title43/chap33/sec1616/).
- Al Ketzler Sr. of Nenana, who organized the 1962 Tanana meeting, is credited with a campaign to halt the state's land selections in 1966 (Tanana Chiefs Conference: https://www.tananachiefs.org/?p=35789).

**What applied in 1967-68.** The administrative freeze: Interior was not processing state selections or other transfers on land under Native protest. Native allotment applications under the 1906 Act could still be filed. Anything the councilman holds must have been patented or entered before the freeze took hold, which fits "decades ago".

**Recommended change.** None needed. Optional texture: Lucy can say the freeze "isn't even a law, just Udall's say-so, and the state is suing to lift it", which is true to the moment and raises the stakes on timing. (Alaska did challenge the freeze in court; verify the case name before using it in dialogue.)

### 2. 1906 Native Allotment Act and proof of use and occupancy

**Claim.** Act 4: "every sign that the family has used that land for generations ... An allotment claim lives or dies on proof of use. Without it, the claim is just paper." The design guesses "likely five years, per the 1956 amendment".

**Verdict.** Partly. The proof requirement and the five-year rule are confirmed. The idea that destroying the camp in 1968 destroys the claim is overstated.

**Evidence.**
- The 1906 Act (34 Stat. 197) allowed up to 160 acres. The Act of August 2, 1956 (70 Stat. 954) added that no allotment be made until the applicant proves "substantially continuous use and occupancy of the land for a period of five years". It was repealed by ANCSA in 1971, with pending applications preserved (BLM: https://blm.gov/programs/lands-and-realty/regional-information/alaska/land_transfer/ak-native-allotment-act; 70 Stat. 954: https://www.govinfo.gov/link/statute/70/954; Alaska DNR legal reference: https://dnr.alaska.gov/mlw/survey/ev/pdf/legalrefs/Highway_ROW_Allotments-07_29_2014.pdf).
- Use may be seasonal, for customary purposes such as fishing (43 C.F.R. 2561.0-5).
- After an application, a BLM field examiner visited the land to look for evidence of use, often by low-flying helicopter, with ground checks if signs of use were seen (*Pence v. Kleppe*, 529 F.2d 135 (9th Cir. 1976): https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F2/529/135/386672/). An Alaska legislative document says early-1970s Interior policy required "clear, physical evidence", which was hard to meet because fishing and hunting leave little trace (https://www.akleg.gov/pdf/billfiles/Committee%20Bill%20Files/1983-1984%20-%2013th%20Legislature/SRES/SRES13%20HB%20455.pdf).
- The use must be the applicant's own personal use, not only ancestral use, and must start before the land was withdrawn or appropriated by someone else (9th Cir.: https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/271/1160/597791/; *Shields v. United States*, 504 F. Supp. 1216 (D. Alaska 1981)).
- Of the 1915 Tanana chiefs who applied for allotments, none lived to see them resolved (ADN on Schneider and Alton's *The Tanana Chiefs*: https://adn.com/arts/books/2019/08/17/how-14-athabascan-tribal-leaders-set-the-course-of-native-rights-in-alaska).

**Analysis.** The game's stakes are real in a practical sense: a BLM examiner flying over in the summer of 1968 who sees no cabin, no racks and no smokehouse is exactly how claims were denied in that era. But the five years of use being proved are in the past; witness statements and old photographs still exist after a flood. The camp's destruction weakens the claim; it does not end it. Two other legal facts matter: (a) the family's use must predate the councilman's homestead entry, and (b) if the councilman already holds a patent, the remedy is a federal action to cancel or recover title, which takes years. The good ending's "The family's allotment is restored" is plausible as an eventual outcome, not as a quick one.

**Recommended change (text tweak).** In Act 4, change "An allotment claim lives or dies on proof of use. Without it, the claim is just paper." to something like: "The BLM man flies over in July. If he sees a smokehouse and racks, the claim is real. If he sees a gravel bar, it's an old woman's word against a councilman's deed." In the good-ending epilogue, say the claim is "reopened" or "won years later", not "restored". Keep graves in the list: they are strong evidence of long use and also raise the moral stakes.

### 3. Route geography, river crossings and gravel

**Claim.** The fish camp "sits on the river crossing and gravel source any future haul road or pipeline north would need". Owen surveys "gravel and river crossings north of Fairbanks"; his camp is "in the hills north of Fairbanks, toward the Yukon crossing". Act 2: "The best crossing on the whole river runs straight through her family's fish camp."

**Verdict.** Partly. Gravel and crossings were real constraints. The specific geography needs care.

**Evidence.**
- The Elliott Highway already ran from Fox to Livengood by 1967; it was built through the 1930s (Olnes to Livengood completed by 1938; Living New Deal: https://livingnewdeal.org/?p=96818). It crosses the Chatanika River at about mile 10.5-11 (https://bridgelookup.com/bridge/16567; The Milepost: https://themilepost.com/routes/elliott-highway/) and the Tolovana at about mile 54.6, where the current bridge dates to 1969 (https://bridgecondition.com/bridge/alaska/ak-0440).
- The Hickel Highway winter road was cut from Livengood to the North Slope via Stevens Village starting November 1968 and finished March 1969 (PBS American Experience: https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/americanexperience/features/pipeline-governor-walter-j-hickel-and-hickel-highway; Alaska State Library photos, "Twenty-five miles north of Livengood", Dec. 1968: https://alaska.libraryhost.com/repositories/2/archival_objects/31543). The permanent haul road started at the end of the Elliott Highway and ran north from the Yukon (Petroleum News: https://www.petroleumnews.com/pnarchpop/222099167.shtml).
- The pipeline route crossed the Chatanika near Olnes, a USGS study site for pipeline river crossings (https://pubs.usgs.gov/of/1978/0611/report.pdf). TAPS needed more than 34 major river crossings and about 800 smaller stream crossings.
- Gravel: the haul road and pipeline drew on many floodplain and upland material sites; GAO later documented fish-passage and floodplain damage from gravel pits along the road (GAO EMD-81-69: https://www.gao.gov/products/emd-81-69). A total gravel volume for the 1972 EIS was not found.
- Minto: in the 1960s the village was still at Old Minto on the Tanana River (it moved to the Tolovana in 1969). Historic Minto fishing sites lay near Minto Lakes and along the Chatanika River, with fishing on the Tolovana (Arctic journal, 1989: https://journalhosting.ucalgary.ca/index.php/arctic/article/download/64732/48646/184123). TCC notes Minto families keep seasonal camps on the Tanana River and Goldstream Creek (https://www.tananachiefs.org/about/communities/minto/).

**Analysis.** A road north from Fairbanks in 1967 would have followed the existing Elliott Highway to Livengood, so the Chatanika and Tolovana crossings already had road bridges. The new crossing a road to Prudhoe needed was the Yukon, near Stevens Village, which is not Lower Tanana country. What a pipeline and a heavy haul road needed nearer Fairbanks was new crossing sites built to higher standards, and gravel: huge amounts, mined from river floodplains. A Minto family's fish camp on a gravel bar or bench of the Chatanika or Tolovana, at or near where a pipeline would cross, is plausible. "The best crossing on the whole river" for a road is weaker, because a road already crossed there.

**Recommended change (plot tweak, small).** Put the fish camp on the lower Chatanika or the Tolovana (both in the Minto fishing area), and make its value the gravel and a pipeline crossing, not the road crossing. Suggested text for Act 2: "The best gravel on the whole river, and the easiest place to put a pipe across it, are both on her family's fish camp." Keep Owen's survey camp toward the Yukon (he was surveying the whole route). Check the river choice with Minto consultants, since camp locations are family knowledge. To settle the exact geography, look at the 1971-72 TAPS route maps and USGS 1:63,360 Livengood and Fairbanks quadrangles.

### 4. Prudhoe Bay timeline

**Claim.** Act 4 opens "March 1968. The radio says it before anybody believes it: oil at Prudhoe Bay, on the Arctic coast, maybe the biggest field in North America." `design.md`: Owen's data is "an early insider read on Prudhoe (the discovery well wasn't drilled out until winter 1967-68 and wasn't public until 1968)".

**Verdict.** Partly. The March date for public news is right. "Maybe the biggest field in North America" in March is premature. Owen holding Prudhoe data in summer 1967 is wrong.

**Evidence.**
- Prudhoe Bay State No. 1 (Atlantic Richfield operator, Humble partner) was spudded April 22, 1967, then shut down for the summer at breakup and resumed after freeze-up in fall 1967 (Petroleum News: https://www.petroleumnews.com/pnarchpop/161706496.shtml).
- Gas flowed on a test on December 27, 1967. Oil shows were logged in February 1968; a press release announced the discovery on February 16, 1968; the first measured oil flow (1,152 barrels a day) came March 12, 1968, with headlines March 13 (Petroleum News archive; Wikipedia summary of the same: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prudhoe_Bay_Oil_Field).
- ARCO's well geologist said "We decided not to make it known until we drilled the confirmation well." The Sag River State No. 1 confirmation was announced June 25, 1968. On July 18, 1968, ARCO and Humble announced the DeGolyer and MacNaughton estimate of 5 to 10 billion barrels; that is when headlines called it giant (Petroleum News, same series).

**Analysis.** In summer 1967, when Owen is surveying and dies, the discovery well was suspended and nobody knew there was oil. A landman buying route land then is betting on the North Slope, not acting on a known field. That actually fits Ashby better: he is speculating ahead of everyone.

**Recommended change.**
- Text tweak: In Act 4, change the radio line to "oil at Prudhoe Bay, on the Arctic coast. Nobody knows how big yet." Optionally add a July epilogue beat: "In July the consultants say five to ten billion barrels. The biggest field in North America."
- Design tweak: Replace "an early insider read on Prudhoe" with "an early bet on the North Slope: the oil men are drilling at Prudhoe that spring and expect to find something, and Owen's route survey is how they plan to get it out". The Act 4 announcement still lands as "oh, that's why".

### 5. Eielson munitions in 1967

**Claim.** Wade is "Air Force munitions" at Eielson; his job is counting "bombs, flares, charges"; the CO forges Wade's name on demolition charge sign-outs; in Act 3 Wade "lifts a demolition kit from the munitions bunker".

**Verdict.** Partly. The job is real and the scheme is plausible, but unit names and where demolition charges were held are unverified.

**Evidence.**
- Munitions specialists held AFSC 461X0 until 1993, when it became 2W0X1; the field covered explosives, bombs, rockets, fuzes, handling, storage, inspection and disposal (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Munitions_Systems_Specialist; AMMO history: https://ammochiefs.com/?p=2593). Munitions maintenance squadrons existed from 1960 onward at many bases.
- At Eielson in 1967 the host unit was the 5010th Combat Support Group (Alaskan Air Command), with a 5010th Consolidated Aircraft Maintenance Squadron. SAC's 4157th Strategic Wing was inactivated March 25, 1967 and replaced by the 6th Strategic Wing (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/5010th_Combat_Support_Group; USAF unit history PDFs at https://ww45.usafunithistory.com/PDF/4000/4157%20STRATEGIC%20WG.pdf). No source named a 1967 munitions squadron at Eielson; munitions there were probably a section of the consolidated maintenance squadron.
- Demolition explosives (TNT, C-4, det cord) are normally held by Explosive Ordnance Disposal teams. In May 1960 the Corps of Engineers had an "Air Force Ordinance Group" blast a Chena River ice jam at Ladd AFB (*Peterson v. United States*, D. Alaska 1963: https://hallapproved.com/ak/cases/federal/district/1963/8755681). So an Air Force unit in the Fairbanks area held demolition charges and used them on ice.
- Losses happen even with modern accountability: an AP investigation found about 50 pounds of Air Force C-4 and 800 feet of det cord went missing without recovery in 2010-2020 (Military Times: https://www.militarytimes.com/news/pentagon-congress/2021/12/02/us-military-explosives-vanish-emerge-in-civilian-world/). No 1960s Eielson case was found.

**Recommended change (text tweak).** Call the charges "demolition blocks" or "TNT and det cord" and put them in the EOD or munitions storage area, with Wade as the airman who handles the paperwork for issues to EOD. Avoid naming a squadron until it is confirmed; "the munitions section" or "Major ___'s squadron" is safe. The forged sign-outs are fine as fiction; one line where Wade notes "anything under a certain weight just gets a signature" would make it feel lived-in. To settle unit names, request the 5010th CSG and 6th Strategic Wing histories for 1967 from the Air Force Historical Research Agency (Maxwell AFB).

### 6. Air Force aerial photography; originals on base

**Claim.** Act 1: Owen's "survey used Air Force aerial photographs. The originals are on your base." Act 2: the photos are "in a records room behind the base photo lab", filed by flight line and frame.

**Verdict.** Partly. The photography existed. "Originals on base" is unlikely.

**Evidence.**
- Army Air Forces flew trimetrogon photography of northern Alaska in 1944 for charting, and wartime charting squadrons flew Alaska each summer until 1943 (ASPRS journal 1953: https://asprs.org/wp-content/uploads/pers/1953journal/jun/1953_jun_391-392.pdf).
- The 46th/72nd Reconnaissance Squadron flew photo mapping from Ladd Field in Fairbanks from 1946 to 1949; strategic reconnaissance then moved to Eielson (https://warhistory.org/article/the-46th-and-72nd-reconnaissance-squadrons-operations-in-the-arctic-1946; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/5001st_Composite_Wing).
- Military single-frame aerial photography of Alaska from the 1940s-50s is now held in the USGS EROS film archive (https://www.usgs.gov/centers/eros/science/usgs-eros-archive-aerial-photography-aerial-photo-single-frames; USGS Alaska metadata for 1948-49 Arctic imagery: https://alaska.usgs.gov/data/metadata/ecosystems/aerialImagery/highAltitude/arcticSlope/Aerial_Images_of_Alaska_Arctic_Coastal_Plain_1948_1949_metadata.html).

**Analysis.** Original negatives went to Air Force charting organizations and then federal archives. A base photo lab at Eielson (a reconnaissance base in the 1960s) could plausibly hold duplicate prints of local mapping flights, or prints from its own more recent missions.

**Recommended change (text tweak).** Change "The originals are on your base" to "The only full set of prints in Alaska is on your base" or "The base photo lab kept prints from the mapping flights." The heist in Act 2 is unchanged.

### 7. The cowboy bowline as a clue

**Claim.** Act 1: the line holding Owen under "is tied off in a cowboy bowline, the backward way your uncle's ranch hands tied them back home". Eli: "Nobody ties himself to a sawhorse." Act 2: Curly ties one on a tarp, "backward, the way the ranch hands did it". Act 3: "Curly ties his bowlines backward, like a Texas ranch hand." `design.md`: "the version of the bowline Texas ranch hands tie (points at a Texan, briefly including Wade)". Handedness is a separate clue (bruising plus the left-handed bow).

**Verdict.** Partly. The knot is real and distinctive enough to notice. The Texas ranch-hand association is folklore at best. The "left-handed bowline" name has nothing to do with left-handed people.

**Evidence.**
- **Real knot, real names.** The bowline with its tail ending outside the loop is catalogued by Clifford Ashley (*The Ashley Book of Knots*, 1944) as the "Left-Hand Bowline", #1034½, which he called "distinctly inferior" because it resembles a left-hand sheet bend. It is also called the cowboy bowline, Dutch bowline, Dutch marine bowline and outside bowline (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cowboy_bowline; https://knots3d.com/en/cowboy-bowline-knot). The Dutch and German navies and some European sailing clubs prefer it (Knots3D).
- **The "cowboy" name.** No source found says where it came from or when it first appeared. One knot-tying site says "cowboy" and "Dutch" do not appear in Ashley or early manuals and that the stories behind them are unverified folklore (The Bear Essentials: https://thebearessentials.com/blogs/knot-tying/how-to-tie-the-cowboy-bowline). Ranch rope work centers on the honda loop of a lariat, not bowlines. No source ties the knot to Texas or to ranch hands. There is a real risk the name "cowboy bowline" was not in common use in 1967.
- **Handedness.** "Left-hand" describes the knot's geometry (its chirality), not the tyer. Knot-tyer discussions agree the label refers to the knot, and that a left-handed person does not automatically tie the left-hand version (IGKT forum: https://discourse.igkt.net/t/knot-forensics-idiosyncrasys-industry-etc/9907; Mountain Project threads cited in search). The game keeps knot and handedness separate, which is correct. Do not merge them.
- **Can an investigator tell it apart on a wet line?** Yes, if the knot is intact. The difference is plain to the eye: the tail sits outside the loop instead of inside. A wet rope does not change that. It could be lost if the knot is cut through, untied, or capsized by the current (bowlines in slick synthetic line can work loose when not loaded). A drowned man pulling against it in current would keep it loaded and set.
- **What forensic knot analysis can bear.** Forensic knot analysis is a real but limited field. Robert Chisnall's studies (*Journal of Forensic Sciences*, 2010, "Knot-Tying Habits, Tier Handedness, and Experience": https://ojp.gov/ncjrs/virtual-library/abstracts/knot-tying-habits-tier-handedness-and-experience) found that people tend to favor one mirror form of basic knots and that unusual tying habits "could reduce the field of possible knot tiers". He also says knot evidence cannot match fingerprints or DNA and there are no accepted standards for knot experts (https://ojp.gov/ncjrs/virtual-library/abstracts/what-knots-can-reveal-strengths-and-limitations-forensic-knot; https://asteetrace.org/knot). An Atlas Obscura piece describes a single case where an expert tied a knot to a butcher's trade, as an anecdote, not a method (https://www.atlasobscura.com/articles/knot-forensics).

**How much weight it can bear.**
- **Strong:** "This was tied, not tangled." Nobody falls into a bowline. This alone justifies Lucy's doubt about the accident ruling. Keep Eli's line.
- **Moderate:** "Whoever tied it has a specific, uncommon habit." Seeing Curly tie the same variant later is a fair-play clue and matches what real knot analysis supports.
- **Weak:** "A Texan tied it." There is no evidence for this association. Players who know knots will call it out, and a player who looks it up will find "Dutch bowline" before "Texas".

**Recommended change (plot and text).**
1. Make the Texas link personal, not regional. Wade knows the knot because one specific foreman on his uncle's ranch tied it that way and taught the hands. Curly worked that same ranch, or one nearby, a few summers back (a line in the prologue's Texas small talk can plant this). Now the knot points at "someone who learned rope where I did", which is narrower and more sinister, and doesn't rest on a regional stereotype. Change Act 3's "like a Texas ranch hand" to "like old Mr. ___ taught the hands on Uncle ___'s place".
2. Drop "cowboy bowline" as a technical term in Wade's narration, or have Wade say the hands called it that. Use "backward bowline, tail on the outside" as the visual description. If a knot-literate character (Eli, who handles lines daily on a riverboat) names it, he would more likely just say "a bowline, tied backward".
3. Add one physical corroborating clue so the case never rests on knot habit alone. Best options, most to least recommended:
   - **Cut-end match.** The line is a distinctive tie-down rope (for example, yellow braided nylon aircraft tie-down line) and its cut end matches a coil in Curly's hangar. Physical matching of cut rope ends is a recognized forensic comparison and gives Barnette something he can take to a grand jury.
   - **The sawhorse.** It came from somewhere: a stenciled hangar sawhorse, or one with aviation dope or engine oil on it.
   - **The skiff.** Eli's witness of a skiff (Twist 5) becomes stronger if the skiff can be identified: a hull patch, a motor, or a borrowed boat Curly returned with a cut bow line.
4. Make Wade preserve the knot. In Act 1, have Wade (or Eli) cut the line above and below the knot rather than untying it, and keep the piece. That makes it evidence instead of a memory, and gives Barnette's "tangled in a line" ruling a concrete thing to ignore.

## Medium priority

### 8. Water-in-fuel icing and sump draining

**Claim.** Act 3: Jake's engine coughs; "Fuel's icing"; he drained the sumps and "nothing came out but a little gas". The sump bowl is solid ice and the drain is jammed with a wired-in sliver of wood. The gasket on the fuel cap was cut.

**Verdict.** Confirmed as plausible.

**Evidence.** NTSB records include Alaska accidents caused by water and ice in fuel; in one, a mechanic tried to drain the sumps before flight and "they would not drain, as though frozen", and the cause was water or ice contamination plus inadequate preflight (NTSB reports via https://data.ntsb.gov/carol-repgen/api/Aviation/ReportMain/GenerateNewestReport/2327/pdf and related entries). A 1993 Nome case found significant ice in the fuel system. Alaska pilots long added isopropyl alcohol to fuel in winter to keep water from freezing (pilot forum anecdotes; treat as texture, not authority).

**Analysis.** One consistency point: in deep cold, water poured into a tank on the ground freezes in the sump before flight, so the sump would not drain at all, which is a warning sign a careful pilot like Jake should catch. The game's jammed drain that lets "a little gas above the plug" out handles this well. It works best if the plane sat in a heated hangar before the flight, so the water stays liquid until altitude.

**Recommended change (text tweak).** Add that the plane was preheated or hangared the night before, which also explains how someone got at the tank. Optional: Jake mentions he added alcohol to the fuel "like always", and Wade finds the can was swapped for water. That gives the sabotage one more fair-play beat.

### 9. Float-to-ski changeover; tundra tires and gravel bars

**Claim.** Act 2: "November. Planes are coming off floats and going onto skis, so nobody is flying anywhere for weeks." Act 1: Owen "flew in and out on tundra tires off a gravel bar the flood has scoured away".

**Verdict.** Partly.

**Evidence.** The seasonal changeover is a defining feature of Alaska flying; clamp-on skis for wheel planes existed from the 1950s (AOPA: https://www.aopa.org/news-and-media/all-news/2006/may/flight-training-magazine/spring-flight-lesson; General Aviation News: https://generalaviationnews.com/2021/01/09/video-from-bush-wheels-to-skis). Freeze-up is the gap between open water and ice thick enough to land on. Large low-pressure tires date back to Goodyear airwheels around 1930 and were long used by Alaska bush pilots under local approvals (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tundra_tire). No source dated "tundra tires" by that name to 1967.

**Recommended change (text tweak).** "Nobody is flying anywhere" is too strong: wheel planes still flew from Fairbanks runways. Change to "nobody can land at a camp with no strip until the ice and snow are good". For Owen, "big tires off a gravel bar" is safer than the term "tundra tires".

### 10. Who ran a 1967 winter crash search

**Claim.** Act 3: "the rescue team out of Eielson needs a munitions man for flares and signal charges."

**Verdict.** Partly.

**Evidence.** In the 1960s, search and rescue for inland Alaska belonged to Alaskan Air Command, directed by the Rescue Coordination Center at Elmendorf, which drew on the Army, Air Force, Civil Air Patrol, state police and volunteers (Air Force Magazine, Sept. 1964: https://www.airandspaceforces.com/PDF/MagazineArchive/Magazine%20Documents/1964/September%201964/0964alaskan.pdf). The 71st Air Rescue Squadron was the Air Force rescue unit in Alaska. A 1970 Alaska Wing report said rescue units relied on Civil Air Patrol for more than 70 percent of missions (https://www.scribd.com/doc/39074943/Alaska-Wing-Annual-Report-1969). No source confirmed a rescue detachment at Eielson in 1967.

**Recommended change (text tweak).** "The Rescue Coordination Center calls Eielson for a ground team" works. Wade's way in (a munitions man for flares and signal charges) is reasonable fiction. A ground party on skis would more likely be Army troops from Fort Wainwright with Air Force support, so having a few soldiers on the team adds realism.

### 11. 1967 Chena flood: responders and death toll

**Claim.** Act 1: Barnette "has six drownings and a town underwater"; "the Air Force has trucked half of Eielson into town to help".

**Verdict.** Confirmed.

**Evidence.** USGS: six lives lost, about 12,000 people evacuated; the Chena crested at Fairbanks August 15, 1967 (USGS Water-Supply Paper 1880-A: https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/wsp1880A). Eielson's 5010th Combat Support Group won an Air Force Outstanding Unit Award for its response to the flood, August 12-21, 1967 (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/5010th_Combat_Support_Group). Fort Wainwright was itself flooded. Minto was also flooded and evacuated to Fairbanks in 1967.

**Recommended change.** None. Optional texture: Eli's family or Minto neighbors being evacuated to Fairbanks during the flood is historical and ties Eli to the event.

### 12. Left-handed hunting bows and 1960s Alaska bowhunting

**Claim.** Act 3: Curly steals "a brand-new hunting bow ... A left-handed one."

**Verdict.** Confirmed.

**Evidence.** Collectors document left-hand Bear recurves from 1967 and a left-hand Bear Grizzly around 1964 (https://tradbow.com/?p=6907; TradGang forum). Bear introduced the Super Kodiak in 1967. Fred Bear himself shot a left-handed 1959 Kodiak recurve when he took his 1962 record Kodiak brown bear (https://www.grandviewoutdoors.com/bear/iconic-1962-video-footage-fred-bears-giant-kodiak-brown-bear), and Boone and Crockett lists a brown bear he took in 1959 on the Little Delta River, in the Interior near Fairbanks (https://www.boone-crockett.org/node/577771).

**Recommended change.** None. Optional: a "brand-new Bear Super Kodiak, left-hand" is period-exact (avoid the trademark in commercial release if that is a concern). Note that shooting hand follows eye dominance as much as handedness, so the bow is good but not airtight evidence; that is fine because it stacks with the bruising.

### 13. Dynamiting ice jams on Interior rivers

**Claim.** Act 4: the CO blows an ice jam above the fish camp at breakup with Eielson charges.

**Verdict.** Confirmed, and better than expected.

**Evidence.** On May 2, 1960, an ice jam formed on the Chena at Ladd AFB in Fairbanks; the Corps of Engineers arranged for "an Air Force Ordinance Group who were experts in this type of work" to blast it, and about fifteen minutes after the last charges, the impounded water and ice moved downstream. Property owners sued (*Peterson v. United States*, D. Alaska 1963: https://hallapproved.com/ak/cases/federal/district/1963/8755681). The Army Air Forces bombed a Yukon ice jam near Galena in 1945 (UAF Geophysical Institute: https://www.gi.alaska.edu/alaska-science-forum/bishop-rocks-oversized-effect-breakup).

**Recommended change.** Use it. A local who remembers "the Air Force blew the Chena jam in '60" gives the CO's act a precedent and a cover story (he could claim he was relieving a jam). The geography in the game (jam upstream, surge floods the camp downstream) matches how a released jam behaves.

### 14. Alaska State Troopers, ranks, jurisdiction, grand jury vs OSI

**Claim.** Barnette is a State Trooper "Inspector"; the force "had just been renamed in 1967"; a Trooper and a Fairbanks PD officer overlap; Barnette takes a case to a state grand jury while "the Air Force's investigators" run their own inquiry.

**Verdict.** Rename confirmed; the rest partly.

**Evidence.** Alaska State Police (1959-1967) became the Alaska State Troopers in 1967 under Governor Hickel (Alaska DPS: https://dps.alaska.gov/AST/History). An Alaska Bureau of Investigations existed within DPS later; its founding date was not confirmed. Alaska has state grand juries under its constitution. Air Force crimes on base go to the Air Force Office of Special Investigations (founded 1948); stolen federal explosives would also be federal crimes. "Inspector" as a 1967 Trooper rank was not verified.

**Recommended change.** Use "Investigator" or "Sergeant" until a 1967 rank list is found (DPS historical rosters or the Alaska State Archives would settle it). Jurisdiction is fine: Troopers cover areas outside city limits (a cabin "on the edge of town" may be outside), Fairbanks PD covers the city.

### 15. E.T. Barnette; "great-grandfather"

**Claim.** Act 1: "his great-grandfather founded this town and skipped out on it with everybody's money."

**Verdict.** Partly.

**Evidence.** Barnette founded a trading post on the Chena on August 26, 1901. His Washington-Alaska Bank failed January 4, 1911, costing depositors about $1 million. He was tried in Valdez and acquitted of embezzlement in December 1912, though his reputation as a swindler stuck, and he left Fairbanks (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E._T._Barnette; Wilmette newspaper item "Alaskan Banker Acquitted": https://history.wilmettelibrary.info/1427446/data; https://finhist.com/bank-runs/episodes/20.html). Barnette was born in 1863 and married in 1898; a great-grandson would be a teenager in 1967. No source on real descendants was found.

**Recommended change (text tweak).** Use "grandfather". Change "skipped out on it with everybody's money" to "skipped out on it after his bank took everybody's money. A jury let him off. Fairbanks never did." Because Barnette is a real person with possible real descendants, keep Vernon clearly fictional, and consider a genealogy check (Alaska State Library or a Fairbanks historian) before release.

### 16. Fairbanks hospital in 1967

**Claim.** Joan works at "the hospital"; Act 3 hospital scenes happen in December 1967 to early 1968.

**Verdict.** Confirmed, with a timeline wrinkle.

**Evidence.** St. Joseph's Hospital (Sisters of Providence) on the Chena was flooded in August 1967; sandbagging failed and patients, doctors and staff were evacuated. The Sisters said they could not afford repairs, and the hospital closed in June 1968. Fairbanks Memorial Hospital opened in March 1972 (Sketches of Alaska: https://www.sketchesofalaska.com/2022/03/st-josephs-hospital-served-fairbanks-medical-needs-for-more-than-50-years.html).

**Recommended change.** Name St. Joseph's. In Act 1, Joan can work in an evacuated ward or a school gym serving as a temporary hospital. By winter, St. Joseph's is back in limited operation and visibly failing, which is good texture. In the epilogue, Joan could lose her job when St. Joseph's closes in June 1968. Whether St. Joseph's reopened in late 1967 was not confirmed; the Fairbanks Daily News-Miner archive would settle it.

### 17. Tanana Chiefs Conference, the 1915 meeting, Minto

**Claim.** `design.md`: TCC "organized in the early 1960s" pressed Interior claims in 1967-68. Act 2: an elder recalls the 1915 meeting with "Judge Wickersham" in Fairbanks, who "told us to take allotments and become citizens". Minto relocated around 1969-71.

**Verdict.** Partly.

**Evidence.**
- TCC grew out of a June 1962 meeting of 32 villages at Tanana organized by Al Ketzler Sr. of Nenana (as Dena' Nena' Henash, "Our Land Speaks"); it was formally incorporated in 1971. Ketzler campaigned against state land selections in 1966 and was president 1962-64 and 1968-72 (https://www.tananachiefs.org/about/our-history/; https://www.tananachiefs.org/?p=35789).
- The Tanana chiefs met in Fairbanks on July 5-6, 1915, at the Thomas Memorial Library, with James Wickersham, then Alaska's Delegate to Congress (he had been a federal judge earlier). He offered reservations or 160-acre allotments; the chiefs rejected reservations; three applied for allotments, and none lived to see them resolved (Alaska Historical Society: https://alaskahistoricalsociety.org/the-1915-tanana-chiefs-meeting/; NPS: https://home.nps.gov/places/thomas-memorial-library.htm; ADN on *The Tanana Chiefs*, 2018). Chief Alexander of Tolovana, from the Minto area, was among them.
- Minto moved from Old Minto on the Tanana to the Tolovana site in 1969 (some sources say 1971) after repeated flooding (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minto,_Alaska; TCC Minto page).

**Recommended change (text tweak).** "Judge Wickersham" is what elders may well have called him (he was widely known as Judge), so it can stay in an elder's mouth; narration should say "Delegate Wickersham" or just "Wickersham". In 1967, call the group "the Tanana Chiefs" (as the game does) rather than "Tanana Chiefs Conference, Inc." The elder's line works better as "He told us to take allotments or take reservations. We took allotments. We have been filling out his papers for fifty years." A Minto elder recalling Chief Alexander of Tolovana would tie the 1915 meeting to Eli's family; leave that to consultants.

### 18. Peacetime military mail reading

**Claim.** The CO reads Wade's mail "off the books".

**Verdict.** Confirmed (the design's assumption is right).

**Evidence.** A military postal historian says censorship of soldiers' mail was dropped after WWII, with some early Korean War carryover, and was not practiced in the Vietnam era (PBS American Experience: https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/americanexperience/features/warletters-censorship/). The Court of Military Appeals treated mail tampering by servicemen as a military offense, separate from the federal statute, until mail enters the Postal Service (*United States v. Scioli*, CMA 1957).

**Recommended change.** None. If the CO is caught, mail tampering adds a charge under the UCMJ.

### 19. Daylight saving time in Fairbanks, summer 1967

**Claim.** The Prologue text (`text-game/src/scenes/prologue.ts`) says "The midnight sun is still up at eleven" in July 1967, and the engine keeps the sun's disc 0 to +4 degrees above the north-northwest horizon at 11 pm (a stated cheat in `art/README.md`).

**Verdict.** Unknown.

**Evidence.** Fairbanks kept Alaska-Hawaii Standard Time (UTC-10) in 1967. The Uniform Time Act of 1966 took effect in 1967 and let a state opt out of daylight saving by law; whether Alaska observed it in summer 1967 is not settled here. Computed for 64.84 N, 147.72 W at 23:00 local: on standard time (UTC-10) the sun is at -1.5 / -3.0 / -6.3 degrees on July 1 / 15 / 31; on daylight time (UTC-9) it is at +0.7 / -0.8 / -4.0 degrees. So the disc is above the horizon at eleven only on daylight time and only in early July; on standard time it is down all month, with the northern sky still bright.

**Recommended change.** The Prologue text depends on the answer: "still up at eleven" is literally true only on daylight time in early July. The engine keeps the disc up either way as an intentional cheat under "plausible, not archival". If the answer is standard time, consider "the sky is still bright at eleven" in the text. Settle it with the Fairbanks Daily News-Miner's sunrise and sunset tables for July 1967.

### 22. Which way does 2nd Avenue run, and can it hold a north-northwest sun?

**Claim.** The `second-ave` target looks roughly northwest along the avenue toward the low midnight sun.

**Verdict.** Unknown.

**Evidence.** Downtown Fairbanks is laid out along the Chena, and 2nd Avenue is usually described as running roughly east-west, parallel to the river. A street running due east-west cannot show a north-northwest sun near its vanishing point; one that bends or runs a little off the grid can.

**Recommended change.** Check the 1967 street grid against a USGS or city map of the period. If 2nd Avenue runs straight east-west, read the target as a cross street looking north toward the river, and stage the in-game sun off the avenue's axis.
